\section{Solver correctness on a solvable game}\label{sec:res-pushfold}

For six players CFR comes with no guarantee (\cref{sec:multiplayer}), so we first check the solver where the theory applies. Forcing the first four seats to fold at the root turns our tree into a two-player game between SB and BB. With stacks of $10\bb$ and no rake it is a two-player zero-sum game in which SB moves all-in or folds and BB calls or folds: the classic \emph{push/fold} game \cite{ganzfried2008}. Its exploitability (\cref{sec:equilibria}) can be computed exactly from the heads-up equity table: for each player, the best response to the opponent's strategy is found class by class, and the exploitability is the average of the two best-response gains.

\Cref{fig:pushfold-expl} shows the exploitability of the average strategy against the number of iterations. Starting from $0.29\bb$ per hand after $10^3$ iterations (a uniformly random strategy gives away $0.50\bb$), it falls to $1.5\times 10^{-5}\bb$ per hand after $10^8$ iterations with linear averaging, a reduction by a factor of about $500$ over the last three decades. This is close to a $1/T$ rate, much faster than the $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ worst-case bound of \cref{sec:cfr}, as is commonly observed in practice. Linear averaging is about $3.4$ times less exploitable than uniform averaging at $10^8$ iterations, because it discounts the poor early iterates. The resulting strategies (\cref{fig:pushfold-grids}) have the familiar shape: SB moves all-in with most hands, BB calls with a tighter range weighted towards pairs and aces.

\section{Convergence and performance of the six-max solve}\label{sec:res-convergence}

For the six-player game we track the \emph{strategy change} between successive chunks of $10^7$ iterations: the reach- and range-weighted total-variation distance between the average strategies at the start and end of the chunk, so frequent spots and frequent hands count most. Without a convergence guarantee, the practical stopping rule is that this number is small and still falling. \Cref{fig:convergence} shows it falling steadily from $6.1\%$ in the second chunk to $0.16\%$ in the last, a factor of about $40$, with no sign of cycling. Throughput is $5.5\times 10^6$ iterations per second on average with eight threads (lower in the first chunk, while the tables are cold), so the full $3\times 10^8$-iteration solve takes about a minute.

A second, model-internal check is indifference: at an equilibrium, every action a hand mixes between has the same value, and the strategy's mix loses nothing against the best action. The trainer's rollouts (\cref{sec:trainer}) estimate exactly these action values for any dealt spot, with standard errors, so a user can spot-check the solution in any hand; we use this as a diagnostic rather than as a systematic metric.

\section{Agreement with the reference charts}\label{sec:res-charts}

\paragraph{Overall.} Against the GTO charts the shipped Scratch solve scores $0.136$ (category-balanced mean distance; $0$ is identical play and $1$ disjoint): $0.085$ for opens, $0.083$ for responses to an open, $0.166$ facing a 3-bet, $0.244$ facing a 4-bet and $0.101$ facing a 5-bet all-in. Against the PTO charts it scores $0.136$ as well. The open and flat/3-bet decisions, which are the most frequent in practice, agree best; the deep 4-bet spots, where every decision commits a large share of the stack and the realization model carries the most weight relative to fold equity, agree worst.

\paragraph{Range charts.} \Cref{fig:grids-a,fig:grids-b} place our ranges next to the GTO charts for six representative spots; \cref{app:charts} has more, including the PTO charts. Cells are filled left to right in proportion to the raise, call and fold frequencies; white cells are hands the player never brings to the spot. The opens agree closely (\cref{fig:grids-a}): the same hands open, and the differences are at the edges of the range. Facing an open, the cold-calling spot BTN versus CO agrees well, while BB versus BTN is among the weakest spots of its category. There our solve 3-bets the weak offsuit aces (A9o--A2o) and the middle pairs (99--66), which the chart flats, and folds offsuit connected hands such as K9o--T9o, which the chart defends. Facing a 3-bet and a 4-bet (\cref{fig:grids-b}) the overall structure matches, value hands raising and the strongest calling, but the frequencies of the marginal hands differ more.

\section{The fixed-strategy fit}\label{sec:res-fit}

This section reports the study of \cref{sec:fixed-fit}: fitting $\theta$ without a solve per candidate, by holding every strategy fixed at the Anchored solve and asking which $\theta$ makes the charts' own mixes look like (quantal) best responses. The study was run with the parameters found by coordinate search (\cref{app:params}) and the Anchored solve made with them.

\paragraph{Gate 1: the sampler.} The sampled records must reproduce the trainer's rollouts, which are computed independently. \Cref{tab:gate1} compares the expected values rebuilt from the records with \texttt{rollout()} for $13$ actions at $8$ spot--class pairs, chosen to include multiway flops and all-ins. The largest discrepancy is $2.2$ combined standard errors, consistent with sampling noise; folds agree exactly. The working sample, at an effective sample size of $5000$ per spot--class pair, holds $2.8\times 10^7$ flop records over \num{1029} flop terminals and \num{5058} spot--class pairs, and is generated in $20$ seconds. The numpy port of the payoff reproduces the C++ payoffs to $7\times 10^{-8}$ of the pot, and one evaluation of the loss and its analytic gradient takes about half a second.

\IfFileExists{data/gate1_table.tex}{\input{data/gate1_table.tex}}{}

\paragraph{The fit.} At temperature $\tau = 1\bb$, L-BFGS-B converges in $42$ iterations ($21$ seconds). The balanced KL divergence falls from $0.770$ to $0.693$ and the charts' regret from $0.462$ to $0.383\bb$ (\cref{tab:fit}). The gains are concentrated in the responses to a 3-bet (regret $0.89 \to 0.54\bb$) and to an open ($0.14 \to 0.08\bb$). Facing a 4-bet nothing improves ($0.91\bb$ before and after), and facing a 5-bet nothing can, since those spots end in all-ins whose payoffs do not involve $\real$. The fitted parameters move consistently across temperatures (\cref{tab:fit-thetas}): the flush, set, domination, low-card and re-raised-domination terms all grow. The exception is the depth half-life $\kappa$, which runs to its lower bound for $\tau \le 1$, switching the depth fade off, but stays near its old value at $\tau = 3$.

\paragraph{Gate 2: full solves.} The fit is only useful if it improves the real objective, the distance of a fresh Scratch solve to the charts. \Cref{tab:gate2} reports full $3\times 10^8$-iteration solves for each candidate. The fitted parameters make the solve \emph{worse}: $0.149$ and $0.151$ at $\tau = 1$, and $0.148$ and $0.145$ at $\tau = 0.3$, against a mean of $0.136$ for the starting point. The per-category breakdown shows why. The fit improves the opens and the responses to an open, as it promised, but the responses to a 4-bet deteriorate from about $0.23$ to $0.29$, which more than cancels the gains.

\IfFileExists{data/gate2_table.tex}{\input{data/gate2_table.tex}}{}

\paragraph{Partial moves.} If the fit points in a useful direction but overshoots, a smaller step may help. Moving a quarter of the way changes nothing measurable. Moving halfway gives $0.1346$ (mean of four seeds) against $0.1364$ for the starting point, an improvement of $0.0018$, about two standard errors, with the smallest seed-to-seed spread of all candidates. Moving all the way except for $\kappa$ gives $0.1350$, with a larger spread. On every seed the same pattern recurs: moving towards the fit helps opens, responses to an open and responses to a 5-bet, and hurts responses to a 4-bet. We adopted the halfway point, the values now shipped.

\paragraph{Why the fit fails.} The fixed-strategy loss asks only whether each chart is a good response to opponents who also play the charts. In a full solve every player adapts to the new $\theta$ at once. Changing the domination and depth terms changes which hands 4-bet, and the players facing a 4-bet then respond to a different range than the chart assumes, so their solved ranges drift away from the charts. The loss is blind to this equilibrium feedback, and the feedback is strongest exactly in the deep spots. A second symptom points the same way: with thirteen parameters the model cannot make the 4-bet charts rational even holding everything else fixed (their KL barely moves), so small local gains elsewhere do not survive re-equilibration. The fixed-strategy fit is therefore a useful search direction, cheap enough to explore, but not a substitute for scoring full solves.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:res-limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A model, not the game.} We solve a preflop game whose postflop value is replaced by thirteen parameters. However well they are fitted, the solution is an equilibrium of the model; agreement with the charts is evidence of a good model, not a proof of equilibrium play in real poker.
  \item \textbf{No guarantee beyond two players.} For six players and with rake, CFR need not converge to a Nash equilibrium (\cref{sec:multiplayer}). We monitor convergence empirically and verify the solver on a two-player game, but cannot certify the six-player solution.
  \item \textbf{Equity simplifications.} The exact heads-up table ignores the cards of folded players, whose hands skew weak; a fixed set of dead cards can move equity by a few points. Multiway equity uses $K = 100$ sampled boards, so the realization-adjusted share carries an $O(1/K)$ bias (\cref{sec:shares-maths}).
  \item \textbf{Abstraction.} One raise size per level, no limping and a single stack depth. These match the reference charts but restrict what the model can express.
  \item \textbf{The reference.} The charts are themselves the output of a solver with its own abstractions; a distance of zero is not the goal in itself, and the PTO charts are deliberately not an equilibrium.
  \item \textbf{Fitting cost.} Each candidate in the coordinate search is a full solve, which limits how many parameters can be fitted, and the cheaper fixed-strategy fit cannot see equilibrium feedback (\cref{sec:res-fit}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and future work}\label{sec:conclusion}

We built a complete pipeline for six-max preflop strategy: a precise preflop game with rake, a realization-adjusted payoff model built from interpretable hand features, an external-sampling Monte Carlo CFR solver that solves the \num{7499}-node tree in about a minute on a laptop, a fit of the model to published charts, and an interactive trainer that grades decisions with importance-sampled rollouts. The solver converges as theory predicts on the heads-up push/fold game, its six-player solve stabilises steadily, and the solution agrees with the published GTO charts to a category-balanced distance of $0.136$, best for opens and responses to an open and worst facing 4-bets. A solve with the charts locked shows where the model and the charts disagree, chiefly in how often weak offsuit aces and broadways should re-raise. A cheaper fixed-strategy fit improved the frequent spots but could not see the equilibrium feedback that governs the deep ones; moving halfway towards it gave a small, consistent improvement.

Several directions follow naturally.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A loss that sees re-equilibration.} The fixed-strategy loss could re-solve the responders' ranges for each candidate, for instance the 4-bettor's range, which would make it track the full-solve objective in the deep spots. That is the precondition for fitting a richer model.
  \item \textbf{Richer realization features.} We have computed per-class features against a random hand (equity, the spread of flop equity, and made-hand and nut-flush frequencies) and the live-player count per terminal is available; a log-linear model $\log\real = w^\top\varphi$ over them is the natural next model once the loss above exists.
  \item \textbf{Postflop ground truth.} Realization factors could be measured directly with a postflop solver on sampled flops and regressed on hand features, using the charts only for validation.
  \item \textbf{Game extensions.} Dead-card-aware equity, a second raise size, SB limping and other stack depths are all supported by the configuration and would bring the game closer to real play.
\end{itemize}
