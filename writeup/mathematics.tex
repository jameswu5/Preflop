\chapter{Mathematical Background}\label{ch:maths}

This chapter covers games and equilibria (\cref{sec:prelim}), counterfactual regret minimisation (CFR) and the
variants we use (\cref{sec:cfr,sec:mccfr,sec:variants}), what survives with more than two players
(\cref{sec:multiplayer}), the estimators inside our payoffs and rollouts (\cref{sec:estimators}), and the fitting of
the payoff model (\cref{sec:fitting-maths}). The poker-specific model is left to \cref{ch:method}: here it is enough
that the game is finite and its payoffs computable.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Extensive-form games}\label{sec:efg}

\begin{definition}[Finite extensive-form game]\label{def:efg}
A finite extensive-form game with chance and imperfect information consists of
\begin{itemize}
\item a finite set of players $\Players = \{1,\dots,n\}$ and a chance player $\chance$;
\item a finite set of \emph{histories}, sequences of actions closed under taking prefixes, with $h \sqsubseteq h'$
  meaning $h$ is a prefix of $h'$. The histories with no extension are the \emph{terminal} histories $\Term$; the
  others are the decision histories $\Hist$, and $\Act(h) = \{a : ha \text{ is a history}\}$ are the legal actions at $h$;
\item a player function $P : \Hist \to \Players \cup \{\chance\}$, and for every $h$ with $P(h) = \chance$ a
  probability distribution $\strat_\chance(h,\cdot)$ on $\Act(h)$;
\item utilities $u_i : \Term \to \Real$ for $i \in \Players$;
\item for each player $i$ an \emph{information partition} $\Info_i$ of $\{h \in \Hist : P(h) = i\}$, such that
  $\Act(h) = \Act(h')$ whenever $h$ and $h'$ lie in the same \emph{information set} $I \in \Info_i$. We write
  $\Act(I)$ for this common set.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

An information set collects the histories that player $i$ cannot tell apart when it is their turn: in poker, those
that differ only in the other players' cards. Player $i$ has \emph{perfect recall} if any two histories in the same
$I \in \Info_i$ pass through the same sequence of $i$'s own information sets and actions: a player never forgets
what they knew or did. All games in this report have perfect recall. In ours an information set is a pair of a public
betting history and the acting player's hand class (\cref{sec:encoding}), and the class does not change during the
hand. The game is \emph{zero-sum} if $\sum_i u_i(z) = 0$ for every $z \in \Term$, and \emph{two-player zero-sum}
(2p0s) if moreover $n = 2$.

\begin{example}[A bluffing game]\label{ex:bluff}
Both players ante one chip. Chance deals player~1 a High or a Low card with probability $\tfrac12$ each. Player~1
checks, ending the hand at a showdown worth $\pm1$, or bets one chip. Facing a bet, player~2, who has not seen the
card, folds (player~1 wins the ante, $+1$) or calls (showdown for $\pm2$). \Cref{fig:toy-game} shows the tree.
Player~1 has two singleton information sets, one per card; player~2 has one information set $I_2$ containing both
histories in which player~1 has bet. The game is 2p0s with perfect recall.
\end{example}

\subsection{Strategies, best responses and equilibria}\label{sec:equilibria}

A \emph{behavioural strategy} $\strat_i$ assigns to each $I \in \Info_i$ a distribution $\strat_i(I) \in
\Delta(\Act(I))$; a \emph{profile} $\strat = (\strat_1,\dots,\strat_n)$ collects one per player, and $\strat_{-i}$
denotes all but player $i$'s. A \emph{mixed} strategy is instead a distribution over pure strategies (a choice of
action at every information set). For games with perfect recall the two are interchangeable: every mixed strategy has
a behavioural strategy that induces the same distribution over terminal histories against every opponent profile,
and conversely (Kuhn's theorem~\cite{kuhn1953}). We therefore work with behavioural strategies throughout.

The \emph{reach probability} of a history under $\strat$ is the product of the probabilities of the actions along it,
\[
  \reach^\strat(h) = \prod_{h'a \sqsubseteq h} \strat_{P(h')}(h',a),
\]
with $\strat_{P(h')}(h',a)$ meaning $\strat_{P(h')}(I,a)$ for the information set $I \ni h'$ (and $\strat_\chance$ at
chance histories). It factors as $\reach^\strat(h) = \reach^\strat_i(h)\,\reach^\strat_{-i}(h)$, player $i$'s own
action probabilities times those of everyone else, chance included. For $h \sqsubseteq z$ we write
$\reach^\strat(h,z)$ for the product over the actions from $h$ to $z$. Under perfect recall, $\reach^\strat_i(h)$ is
the same for all $h \in I \in \Info_i$, and we write it $\reach^\strat_i(I)$. Expected utility is
$u_i(\strat) = \sum_{z \in \Term} \reach^\strat(z)\,u_i(z)$.

\begin{definition}[Best response, equilibrium, exploitability]\label{def:equilibrium}
A strategy $\strat_i^*$ is a \emph{best response} to $\strat_{-i}$ if $u_i(\strat_i^*,\strat_{-i}) =
\max_{\strat_i'} u_i(\strat_i',\strat_{-i})$. A profile is an \emph{$\epsilon$-equilibrium} if no player can gain more
than $\epsilon$ by deviating unilaterally, $u_i(\strat_i',\strat_{-i}) \le u_i(\strat) + \epsilon$ for all $i$ and
$\strat_i'$, and a \emph{Nash equilibrium} if this holds with $\epsilon = 0$. The \emph{exploitability} of $\strat$
is the total gain available to unilateral deviations,
\[
  \expl(\strat) = \sum_{i \in \Players} \Big( \max_{\strat_i'} u_i(\strat_i',\strat_{-i}) - u_i(\strat) \Big) \ge 0.
\]
\end{definition}

Every finite game has a Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies~\cite{nash1951}, hence by Kuhn's theorem one in
behavioural strategies. A profile with $\expl(\strat) \le \epsilon$ is an $\epsilon$-equilibrium, and an
$\epsilon$-equilibrium has $\expl(\strat) \le n\epsilon$. In 2p0s games the picture is much stronger.

\begin{theorem}[Minimax~\cite{vonneumann1928}]\label{thm:minimax}
In a finite 2p0s game,
\[
  \max_{\strat_1}\min_{\strat_2} u_1(\strat_1,\strat_2) = \min_{\strat_2}\max_{\strat_1} u_1(\strat_1,\strat_2) =: v^*,
\]
the \emph{value} of the game. A profile is a Nash equilibrium if and only if each
strategy guarantees the value to its player, and equilibrium strategies are interchangeable.
\end{theorem}

In a 2p0s game $u_1(\strat) + u_2(\strat) = 0$, so exploitability no longer involves $u(\strat)$:
$\expl(\strat) = \max_{\strat_1'} u_1(\strat_1',\strat_2) - \min_{\strat_2'} u_1(\strat_1,\strat_2')$. It is zero
exactly at equilibrium, and it bounds how much either strategy can lose against any opponent relative to the value.
This is what makes ``solving'' a 2p0s game well defined, and it is the guarantee that is lost with more players
(\cref{sec:multiplayer}).

\begin{example}[\Cref{ex:bluff} continued]\label{ex:bluff-eq}
With High, betting is worth at least $+1$ whatever player~2 does and checking exactly $+1$, so player~1 bets.
Suppose player~1 bets Low with probability $p$ and player~2 calls with probability $q$. Facing a bet, player~2
holds the posterior $\Prob(\text{High} \mid \text{bet}) = 1/(1+p)$; calling is worth $(2p - 2)/(1+p)$ to player~2
and folding $-1$, so player~2 is indifferent when $p = \tfrac13$. Player~1 with Low is indifferent between checking
($-1$) and betting ($1 - 3q$) when $q = \tfrac23$. The equilibrium is to bluff a third of the time and call two thirds
of the time, and the value to player~1 is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac53 + \tfrac12\cdot(-1) = \tfrac13$. Two features recur
in poker: mixing makes the opponent indifferent, and the bluffing frequency is pinned down by the size of the bet
relative to the pot.
\end{example}

\subsection{Regret and online learning}\label{sec:regret}

CFR is built from a simple online learning algorithm. At rounds $t = 1, 2, \dots$ a learner picks a distribution
$x^t \in \Delta(\Act)$ over a finite set of actions, after which a reward vector $u^t \in \Real^{\Act}$ with
$\max_a u^t(a) - \min_a u^t(a) \le \Delta$ is revealed, possibly chosen by an adversary. Given nonnegative weights
$w_t$, the instantaneous and cumulative \emph{(external) regrets} are
\[
  r^t(a) = u^t(a) - \langle x^t, u^t\rangle, \qquad R^T(a) = \sum_{t=1}^T w_t\, r^t(a):
\]
how much better the learner would have done by always playing $a$. The plain case is $w_t = 1$. An algorithm is
\emph{no-regret} if $\max_a R^T(a) / \sum_t w_t \to 0$ for every reward sequence.

\emph{Regret matching} plays in proportion to positive cumulative regret,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rm}
  x^{T+1}(a) = \frac{R^T_+(a)}{\sum_{a'} R^T_+(a')} \quad \text{if the denominator is positive, and uniformly otherwise,}
\end{equation}
with $y_+ = \max(y,0)$. It is Blackwell's approachability strategy~\cite{blackwell1956} for the negative orthant, as
observed by Hart and Mas-Colell~\cite{hart2000}; see~\cite{cesabianchi2006} for the general theory. The bound is
short to prove.

\begin{theorem}[Regret matching]\label{thm:rm}
Regret matching guarantees $\max_a R^T(a) \le \Delta \sqrt{|\Act|}\,\big(\sum_{t} w_t^2\big)^{1/2}$. In particular
the average regret is at most $\Delta\sqrt{|\Act|/T}$ when $w_t = 1$, and $O(\Delta\sqrt{|\Act|/T})$ when $w_t = t$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First, $\sum_a R^T_+(a)\, r^{T+1}(a) = 0$: this is trivial if $R^T_+ = 0$, and otherwise, writing $\Sigma =
\sum_a R^T_+(a)$ so that $x^{T+1} = R^T_+/\Sigma$, it equals $\sum_a R^T_+(a) u^{T+1}(a) - \Sigma\,\langle x^{T+1},
u^{T+1}\rangle = 0$. Second, $\big((y + z)_+\big)^2 \le (y_+ + z)^2$ for real $y, z$ (if $y + z > 0$ then
$0 < y + z \le y_+ + z$). Applying it componentwise,
\begin{align*}
  \lVert R^{T+1}_+ \rVert^2 \le \lVert R^T_+ + w_{T+1} r^{T+1} \rVert^2
  &= \lVert R^T_+\rVert^2 + 2 w_{T+1} \langle R^T_+, r^{T+1} \rangle + w_{T+1}^2 \lVert r^{T+1}\rVert^2 \\
  &\le \lVert R^T_+\rVert^2 + w_{T+1}^2 |\Act| \Delta^2,
\end{align*}
since $|r^t(a)| \le \Delta$. By induction $\lVert R^T_+\rVert^2 \le |\Act|\Delta^2 \sum_t w_t^2$, and $\max_a R^T(a)
\le \lVert R^T_+\rVert$. For $w_t = t$, $(\sum_t t^2)^{1/2} / \sum_t t \sim (2/\sqrt3)\,T^{-1/2}$.
\end{proof}

No-regret learning yields equilibria in 2p0s games by self-play. For extensive-form games one must be careful about
what ``average strategy'' means: averaging behavioural strategies information set by information set does not average
the induced play. The right average weights each iterate by the player's own probability of reaching the
information set,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:avgstrat}
  \avgstrat_i(I,a) = \frac{\sum_{t} w_t\, \reach^{\strat^t}_i(I)\, \strat^t_i(I,a)}{\sum_t w_t\, \reach^{\strat^t}_i(I)}.
\end{equation}
By induction along $h$, $\reach^{\avgstrat}_i(h)$ is then the $w$-weighted average of the $\reach^{\strat^t}_i(h)$, and
since utility is linear in a player's reach probabilities, $u_j(\avgstrat_i,\strat_{-i}) = \sum_t w_t
u_j(\strat^t_i,\strat_{-i}) / \sum_t w_t$ for every $\strat_{-i}$ and every $j$.

\begin{proposition}[Folk theorem for 2p0s]\label{prop:folk}
In a 2p0s game, let both players choose $\strat^t_i$ so that their average regret against the sequence of opponent
strategies is at most $\epsilon$,
$\max_{\strat_i'} \sum_t w_t \big(u_i(\strat_i',\strat^t_{-i}) - u_i(\strat^t)\big) \le \epsilon \sum_t w_t$. Then
$\expl(\avgstrat) \le 2\epsilon$ for the average profile \eqref{eq:avgstrat}; in particular $\avgstrat$ is a
$2\epsilon$-equilibrium.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\E_t$ for the $w$-weighted average over $t$. By linearity and the regret bound for player~1, for any
$\strat_1'$, $u_1(\strat_1',\avgstrat_2) = \E_t\, u_1(\strat_1',\strat_2^t) \le \E_t\, u_1(\strat^t) + \epsilon$.
Likewise for player~2, using $u_2 = -u_1$: $u_1(\avgstrat_1,\strat_2') = \E_t\, u_1(\strat_1^t,\strat_2') \ge
\E_t\, u_1(\strat^t) - \epsilon$. Subtracting, $\max_{\strat_1'} u_1(\strat_1',\avgstrat_2) - \min_{\strat_2'}
u_1(\avgstrat_1,\strat_2') \le 2\epsilon$, which is $\expl(\avgstrat)$. Since $u_1(\avgstrat)$ lies between the two
terms, neither player gains more than $2\epsilon$ by deviating.
\end{proof}

\section{Counterfactual regret minimisation}\label{sec:cfr}

Running regret matching on the pure strategies of an extensive-form game is hopeless: there are exponentially many.
CFR~\cite{zinkevich2007} instead runs one regret minimiser at every information set, on a local notion of value. For
$I \in \Info_i$, the \emph{counterfactual value}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cfv}
  \cfv_i^\strat(I) = \sum_{h \in I} \reach^\strat_{-i}(h) \sum_{z \sqsupseteq h} \reach^\strat(h,z)\, u_i(z)
\end{equation}
is player $i$'s expected payoff from $I$ onwards, weighted by the probability that chance and the other players bring
play to each $h \in I$, as if $i$ had played to reach $I$. We write $\cfv_i^\strat(I,a)$ for the same quantity with
action $a$ forced at $I$, so that $\cfv_i^\strat(I) = \sum_a \strat_i(I,a)\,\cfv_i^\strat(I,a)$. The cumulative
\emph{counterfactual regret} after $T$ iterations is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cfr-regret}
  R^T(I,a) = \sum_{t=1}^T w_t \big( \cfv_i^{\strat^t}(I,a) - \cfv_i^{\strat^t}(I) \big),
\end{equation}
and CFR sets $\strat^{T+1}(I)$ by regret matching \eqref{eq:rm} on $R^T(I,\cdot)$. Each information set is thus an
instance of \cref{sec:regret} with reward vector $\cfv_i^{\strat^t}(I,\cdot)$, whose range is at most $\Delta_i =
\max_z u_i(z) - \min_z u_i(z)$ because the weights $\reach_{-i}$ sum to at most one.

\begin{theorem}[Zinkevich et al.~\cite{zinkevich2007}]\label{thm:cfr}
Player $i$'s overall regret is bounded by the sum of the positive counterfactual regrets,
$\max_{\strat_i'} \sum_t w_t\big(u_i(\strat_i',\strat^t_{-i}) - u_i(\strat^t)\big) \le \sum_{I \in \Info_i}
\max_a R^T_+(I,a)$. With regret matching at every information set and $w_t = 1$, the average overall regret is
therefore at most $\Delta_i\, |\Info_i| \sqrt{|\Act_i|} / \sqrt T$, where $|\Act_i|$ is the largest number of actions at
any of $i$'s information sets.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Fix a deviation $\strat_i'$. The gain from it decomposes over $i$'s information sets in a top-down order: switching
from $\strat_i^t$ to $\strat_i'$ at $I$ while keeping $\strat_i'$ below changes the counterfactual value at $I$ by a
term bounded by the local regret there, plus the gains at the information sets that follow, each weighted by
$\strat_i'(I,a) \le 1$. Perfect recall makes the counterfactual values of successive information sets compose,
because $\reach_{-i}$ does not depend on $i$'s own choices. Unrolling the recursion gives the first bound, and
\cref{thm:rm} at each information set gives the second.
\end{proof}

Combining \cref{thm:cfr} with \cref{prop:folk}, in a 2p0s game the average profile of CFR, weighted by each player's
own reach as in \eqref{eq:avgstrat}, is an $\epsilon$-equilibrium with $\epsilon = O(1/\sqrt T)$. The argument goes
through unchanged for nonnegative weights $w_t$, with the factor of \cref{thm:rm} in place of $\sqrt T$; this is how
the linear weights of \cref{sec:variants} keep the guarantee.

\section{Monte Carlo CFR}\label{sec:mccfr}

Each CFR iteration evaluates \eqref{eq:cfv} exactly, which means a pass over the whole tree including every deal of
the cards. With six players there are $52!/(2^6\,40!)$ deals, so this is out of the question. Monte Carlo CFR
(MCCFR)~\cite{lanctot2009} replaces the counterfactual values by unbiased estimates computed on a sampled part of the
tree. If terminal $z$ is sampled with probability $q(z) > 0$, the sampled counterfactual value
\[
  \tilde\cfv_i^\strat(I) = \sum_{h \in I}\ \sum_{\substack{z \sqsupseteq h\\ z \text{ sampled}}}
  \frac{\reach^\strat_{-i}(h)\,\reach^\strat(h,z)}{q(z)}\, u_i(z)
\]
is unbiased for $\cfv_i^\strat(I)$, and regret matching on sampled regrets keeps a regret bound of the same order with
high probability, with constants that grow with the variance of the estimates~\cite{lanctot2009}. Two sampling schemes
are standard. \emph{Outcome sampling} follows a single path from the root to one terminal. \emph{External sampling},
which we use, singles out a \emph{traverser} $i$ per iteration: at chance and opponent histories it samples one action
from the actual distribution $\strat^t$, and at the traverser's histories it enumerates every action. The importance
weight then disappears.

\begin{proposition}[External sampling has importance weight one]\label{prop:es-unbiased}
In one external-sampling pass for traverser $i$, at most one history of each $I \in \Info_i$ is visited. Let
$\tilde\cfv(I,a)$ be the value the recursion of \cref{alg:es-mccfr} returns from the child $ha$ of the visited $h \in
I$ (zero if no $h \in I$ is visited), and $\tilde\cfv(I) = \sum_a \strat^t(I,a)\,\tilde\cfv(I,a)$. Then
$\E\,\tilde\cfv(I,a) = \cfv_i^{\strat^t}(I,a)$, so the sampled regret increment $\tilde\cfv(I,a) - \tilde\cfv(I)$ is
unbiased for $\cfv_i^{\strat^t}(I,a) - \cfv_i^{\strat^t}(I)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Two histories of $I$ agree on $i$'s own actions (perfect recall), so they first differ at a chance or opponent
history, where only one action is sampled; hence at most one is visited. A history $h$ is visited exactly when every
chance and opponent action on the way to it is sampled, which has probability $\reach^{\strat^t}_{-i}(h)$ since the
traverser's actions are all enumerated. Given that, the recursion returns
\[
  \tilde\cfv(I,a) = \sum_{z \sqsupseteq ha} \reach^{\strat^t}_i(ha,z)\, u_i(z)\,
  \1[\text{every non-}i\text{ action from } ha \text{ to } z \text{ is sampled}]:
\]
the traverser's actions are averaged with their own probabilities, and the others' are realised by sampling. The
indicator has conditional expectation $\reach^{\strat^t}_{-i}(ha,z)$. Summing over the disjoint events $\{h
\text{ visited}\}$, $h \in I$,
\[
  \E\,\tilde\cfv(I,a) = \sum_{h\in I} \reach^{\strat^t}_{-i}(h) \sum_{z \sqsupseteq ha}
  \reach^{\strat^t}_i(ha,z)\,\reach^{\strat^t}_{-i}(ha,z)\, u_i(z) = \cfv_i^{\strat^t}(I,a).
\]
In the notation above $q(z) = \reach^{\strat^t}_{-i}(z)$, so each sampled term carries the weight
$\reach_{-i}(h)\reach(h,z)/q(z) = \reach_i(h,z)$, a probability of the traverser's own play: there is no importance
weight. Linearity gives the statement for the regret increment.
\end{proof}

The proposition assumes exact terminal utilities. Ours are Monte Carlo estimates, unbiased at all-ins but slightly
biased at flop terminals (\cref{prop:share-bias}), so there the unbiasedness is approximate.

\paragraph{Averaging at opponent histories.}
External sampling never computes $\reach^{\strat^t}_p(I)$, which \eqref{eq:avgstrat} needs. A common remedy, which we
follow, adds $w_t\strat^t(I)$ to a strategy sum $S(I)$ whenever $I \in \Info_p$ is visited during another player's
traversal, and outputs $\avgstrat(I) = S(I)/\lVert S(I)\rVert_1$. In a pass with traverser $i \ne p$, a history $h \in
I$ is visited with probability $\reach^{\strat^t}_{-i}(h) = \reach^{\strat^t}_p(I)\,\reach^{\strat^t}_{-\{i,p\}}(h)$,
so the expected increment is
\[
  w_t\, \reach^{\strat^t}_p(I)\, c_t(I)\, \strat^t(I), \qquad c_t(I) = \sum_{h\in I}\reach^{\strat^t}_{-\{i,p\}}(h),
\]
averaged over the traverser. This is the textbook weight times $c_t(I)$, the probability that chance and the players
other than $p$ and the traverser lead to $I$. If $c_t(I)$ were constant in $t$ it would cancel on normalisation. It
changes only through the other players' strategies, so it drifts more and more slowly as they settle. An information
set whose strategy sum stays zero has never been visited during another player's traversal, and we output its
regret-matching strategy instead.

\Cref{alg:es-mccfr} is our traversal with the linear block weights of \cref{sec:variants}, and \cref{fig:es-traversal}
shows one pass. The root is the chance node of the deal: six hands are drawn once per iteration, and the remaining
chance (the boards) is handled inside the payoff estimate (\cref{sec:estimators}). The traverser cycles through the
seats.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{External-sampling MCCFR with block-linear weights. $R$ and $S$ are the regret and strategy-sum tables,
initialised to zero, and $N$ is the block length.}\label{alg:es-mccfr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Traverse}{$h, i, w$}
  \If{$h \in \Term$}
    \State \Return $\tilde u_i(h)$ \Comment{sampled payoff, \cref{sec:estimators}}
  \EndIf
  \State $p \gets P(h)$;\quad $I \gets$ the information set of $p$ containing $h$
  \State $\strat(I,\cdot) \gets$ regret matching \eqref{eq:rm} on $R(I,\cdot)$
  \If{$p = i$}
    \For{$a \in \Act(I)$}
      \State $\tilde\cfv(a) \gets \Call{Traverse}{ha, i, w}$
    \EndFor
    \State $\tilde\cfv \gets \sum_{a} \strat(I,a)\,\tilde\cfv(a)$
    \For{$a \in \Act(I)$}
      \State $R(I,a) \gets R(I,a) + w\,\big(\tilde\cfv(a) - \tilde\cfv\big)$
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $\tilde\cfv$
  \Else
    \State $S(I,\cdot) \gets S(I,\cdot) + w\,\strat(I,\cdot)$ \Comment{averaging at opponent histories}
    \State sample $a \sim \strat(I,\cdot)$
    \State \Return \Call{Traverse}{$ha, i, w$}
  \EndIf
\EndFunction
\Statex
\For{$t = 1, \dots, T$}
  \State sample a deal $\omega$ of six hands \Comment{chance, from its own distribution}
  \State $i \gets t \bmod n$;\quad $w \gets \lfloor (t-1)/N \rfloor + 1$ \Comment{the block number}
  \State \Call{Traverse}{$\omega, i, w$}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\avgstrat(I) = S(I)/\lVert S(I)\rVert_1$ for every $I$ (regret matching where $S(I) = 0$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Weighted averaging and other variants}\label{sec:variants}

Vanilla CFR weights every iteration equally, so the early iterations, played against nearly uniform opponents, weigh
on the average for a long time. Several variants change this. CFR+~\cite{tammelin2014} floors the stored regrets at
zero after every update (\emph{regret matching}$^+$), alternates the players' updates and weights the average
linearly; it was the algorithm behind the essential solution of heads-up limit hold'em~\cite{bowling2015}. Brown and
Sandholm's Discounted CFR~\cite{brown2019discounted} multiplies the accumulated positive and negative regrets by
$t^\alpha/(t^\alpha+1)$ and $t^\beta/(t^\beta+1)$, and the strategy sum by $(t/(t+1))^\gamma$, after iteration $t$.
Its simplest case, \emph{Linear CFR}, weights the contributions of iteration $t$ to both tables by $t$. We apply Linear
CFR a block at a time.

\begin{proposition}[Block discounting]\label{prop:linear-blocks}
Split the iterations into blocks of length $N$, block $j$ being $(j-1)N < t \le jN$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If at the end of each block $k$ both tables are multiplied by $k/(k+1)$, then after $K$ blocks the increments
  of block $j$ carry the weight $\prod_{k=j}^{K} \frac{k}{k+1} = \frac{j}{K+1}$, proportional to $j$.
\item If instead each increment made during block $j$ is multiplied by $j$ and the tables are never rescaled, then at
  every iteration of block $k$ the tables equal $k$ times those of~(a), and after the last block $K+1$ times. Both
  schemes produce the same strategies $\strat^t$ and the same average $\avgstrat$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) The product telescopes. (b) Let $\Delta_j$ be the sum of the raw increments of block $j$ and $\Delta'_k$ those made
so far in block $k$. By~(a), during block $k$ the rescaled table holds $D = \sum_{j<k} \frac{j}{k}\Delta_j +
\Delta'_k$, and the lazy table holds $\sum_{j<k} j\,\Delta_j + k\,\Delta'_k = k D$. Regret matching \eqref{eq:rm} and
the normalisation $S(I)/\lVert S(I)\rVert_1$ are invariant under multiplying their input by a positive constant, so
the two schemes play identical strategies at every iteration (given the same random numbers) and output the same
average. By \cref{thm:rm} with $w_t = \lceil t/N \rceil$, the regret bound keeps the order $O(1/\sqrt T)$.
\end{proof}

The lazy form~(b) is what \cref{alg:es-mccfr} does through $w$. It needs no pass over the tables and no
synchronisation between threads (\cref{sec:solver}).

\paragraph{Why we do not floor regrets.}
Flooring interacts badly with sampled regrets. Given the current table entry $Q$ and an unbiased but noisy increment
$\tilde r$ with mean $r$, Jensen's inequality for the convex map $y \mapsto y_+$ gives $\E\,(Q + \tilde r)_+ \ge (Q +
r)_+$: the floored update is biased upwards. The bias is largest for actions whose true regret hovers near zero, which
are exactly the ones an equilibrium mixes or barely rejects. Without the floor, negative regret acts as a memory: an
action that was bad for a long time must earn its way back before it is played again, and noise in either direction
averages out. Linear and Discounted CFR keep negative regrets (DCFR merely discounts them), and that is the family we
stay in.

\section{More than two players}\label{sec:multiplayer}

\Cref{thm:rm,thm:cfr} are statements about one learner against an arbitrary sequence of rewards, so they hold for
each player in any game: CFR still drives every player's average regret to zero. What fails beyond 2p0s is
\cref{prop:folk}, whose proof used $u_2 = -u_1$ to tie the two players' guarantees together. Nothing replaces it. What
no-regret self-play does deliver in general is a weaker, correlated notion.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cce}
If every player's average regret over $T$ iterations is at most $\epsilon$, the joint distribution $\mu$ that draws
$t$ with probability $w_t/\sum_s w_s$ and plays $\strat^t$ is an $\epsilon$-coarse correlated equilibrium: for every
$i$ and $\strat_i'$, $\E_{\mu}\,u_i(\strat_i',\strat_{-i}^t) \le \E_\mu\, u_i(\strat^t) + \epsilon$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Multiplied by $\sum_t w_t$, the displayed inequality is the regret bound.
\end{proof}

A coarse correlated equilibrium is a mixture of profiles, not a profile; internal-regret dynamics give the
stronger correlated equilibria~\cite{hart2000}, and Farina et al.~\cite{farina2020coarse} study coarse correlation in
extensive-form games. The profile $\avgstrat$ of per-player averages that CFR outputs is in general no equilibrium of
any kind, and even Nash equilibria need not be unique or interchangeable, so ``the'' equilibrium strategy of a seat is
not well defined. The practical evidence is nonetheless strong. Pluribus~\cite{brown2019pluribus} computed the blueprint strategy for six-player no-limit hold'em
with Monte Carlo CFR and beat professional players, and Gibson~\cite{gibson2014} documents CFR agents performing well
in three-player poker.

Rake makes the game negative-sum, $\sum_i u_i(z) = -\rake(z)$ (\cref{sec:shares-maths}). Adding the house as a
passive player with utility $\rake(z)$ restores zero-sum, but with seven players, so it buys no guarantee; even
heads-up with rake is outside \cref{prop:folk}, which is why our exact checks run with rake off. With no theorem to
lean on, we check convergence empirically (\cref{sec:res-convergence}).

\section{Estimators used in this work}\label{sec:estimators}

\subsection{Equity}\label{sec:equity-maths}

At a terminal reached before the flop, let $L$ be the set of players still in the hand. Given the hole cards, a board
$B$ of five cards is drawn uniformly from the remaining deck (\cref{sec:equity-impl} says exactly which cards are
removed), and $W(B) \subseteq L$ is the set of players with the best seven-card hand on $B$. The \emph{equity} of $i
\in L$ is the expected share of the pot $i$ would win at showdown with no further betting,
\[
  \eq_i = \E_B\!\left[\frac{\1[i \in W(B)]}{|W(B)|}\right], \qquad \sum_{i\in L} \eq_i = 1.
\]
Heads-up, $\eq_i$ is an exact sum over the $\binom{48}{5}$ boards, which we precompute for every pair of hands. With
three or more players we use the Monte Carlo estimator over $K$ independent boards,
\[
  \widehat\eq_i = \frac1K \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\1[i \in W(B_k)]}{|W(B_k)|},
\]
which is unbiased with variance at most $1/(4K)$, since each term lies in $[0,1]$. At an all-in we use $K = 1$ and pay
the actual pot share on that board. The payoff estimate is then unbiased, and the noise averages out over iterations.

\subsection{Realization-adjusted pot shares}\label{sec:shares-maths}

Without an all-in, the hand would continue to the flop, and raw equity is a poor proxy for what a hand wins there:
position, initiative and playability let some hands win more than their share and others less. We model this with a
\emph{realization factor} $\real_i > 0$ per live player: an abstract positive function of the player's hand class,
role (position, caller or raiser) and the depth of the pot, with parameters $\theta$. Its concrete form is the subject
of \cref{sec:realization}. Pot shares are realization-adjusted equities renormalised over $L$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:share}
  s_i = \frac{\real_i\, \eq_i}{\sum_{j\in L} \real_j\, \eq_j}, \qquad
  u_i(z) = s_i\,\big(\pot(z) - \rake(z)\big) - C_i(z),
\end{equation}
with $s_i = 0$ for $i \notin L$, where $C_i(z)$ is player $i$'s contribution to the pot and $\rake(z)$ the rake.
Since $\sum_i C_i = \pot$, the utilities sum to $-\rake(z)$ at every terminal and for every board. The same payoff
form covers the other terminals, with $s_i = 1$ for the last player when everyone else folds and $s_i = \eq_i$ at an
all-in.

The shares have three properties we use.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Scale invariance.} Multiplying every $\real_j$ by the same $c > 0$ leaves every $s_i$ unchanged. Only
  ratios of realization factors matter, so one parameter can be fixed by convention.
\item \emph{Consistency.} If all $\real_j$ are equal, $s_i = \eq_i$.
\item \emph{One board is not enough.} With $K = 1$ and no tie, $\widehat\eq_j = \1[j \text{ wins}]$, and
  \eqref{eq:share} collapses to $s_i = \1[i\text{ wins}]$ whatever the $\real_j$: the realization model has no effect.
  A flop terminal therefore needs a genuine equity estimate, exact heads-up and with $K$ boards multiway.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{proposition}[Bias of the multiway share]\label{prop:share-bias}
Let $f(e) = \real_i e_i / \sum_j \real_j e_j$, and let $\widehat\eq$ average $K$ independent single-board share
vectors with covariance $\Sigma_1$. Then $\E f(\widehat\eq) = f(\eq) + \frac{1}{2K}\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\nabla^2
f(\eq)\, \Sigma_1\big) + o(1/K)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$f$ takes values in $[0,1]$ and is smooth wherever $\sum_j \real_j e_j > 0$, in particular near $\eq$. A second-order
Taylor expansion about $\eq$, with $\E(\widehat\eq - \eq) = 0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\eq) = \Sigma_1/K$,
gives the leading terms. The remainder is $o(1/K)$ because $\widehat\eq$ concentrates around $\eq$ exponentially fast
(Hoeffding's inequality) and $f$ is bounded; this is the delta method~\cite{owen2013}.
\end{proof}

The share is a ratio of noisy estimates, so it is biased, but only at order $1/K$. Heads-up the exact table removes
the bias altogether. We choose $K$ by checking that the solution stops moving as $K$ grows.

\subsection{Self-normalised importance sampling}\label{sec:snis}

The trainer (\cref{sec:trainer}) and the fixed-strategy fit (\cref{sec:fixed-fit}) need the expected value of each
action for a given hand of one player, the \emph{hero}, at a given spot, assuming everyone plays a fixed strategy from
there on. The spot is reached through a history $h$, which makes some deals of the opponents' cards more likely than
others. Deals are easy to draw uniformly from the cards the hero does not hold (the proposal $q$), but the target is
the conditional distribution given $h$,
\[
  p(\omega) = \frac{q(\omega)\,\ell(\omega)}{Z}, \qquad \ell(\omega) = \prod_{h'a' \sqsubseteq h,\ P(h') \ne \text{hero}}
  \strat\big(h', \bucket(\omega_{P(h')})\big)(a'), \qquad Z = \E_q\,\ell,
\]
where $\ell$ is the probability that the other players' strategies produce the observed actions with the cards
$\omega$, and $\bucket(\omega_p)$ is the class of player $p$'s hand. $Z$ is unknown, which is exactly the setting of
\emph{self-normalised importance sampling}~\cite{owen2013}. With deals $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_N \sim q$, weights
$\ell_n = \ell(\omega_n)$, and a playout payoff $u_n(a)$ for action $a$ on deal $n$, the estimator and its standard
error are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:snis}
  \widehat{\mathrm{EV}}(a) = \frac{\sum_{n} \ell_n\, u_n(a)}{\sum_n \ell_n}, \qquad
  \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(a) = \frac{\big(\sum_n \ell_n^2 \big(u_n(a) - \widehat{\mathrm{EV}}(a)\big)^2\big)^{1/2}}{\sum_n \ell_n}.
\end{equation}
By the strong law of large numbers, numerator and denominator divided by $N$ converge to $\E_q[\ell u] = Z\,\E_p[u]$
and $\E_q[\ell] = Z$, so the estimator is consistent. Being a ratio, it is biased at order $1/N$. The delta method gives
$\sqrt N(\widehat{\mathrm{EV}} - \E_p u) \Rightarrow \mathcal N\big(0, \E_q[\ell^2 (u-\E_p u)^2] / Z^2\big)$, and
$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}^2$ is the plug-in estimate of that variance over $N$.

Precision is governed by the weights, not by $N$. Kish's \emph{effective sample size}~\cite{kish1965}
\[
  N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{\big(\sum_n \ell_n\big)^2}{\sum_n \ell_n^2} \in [1, N]
\]
is the number of equally weighted draws that would give about the same variance, $\operatorname{Var}
\widehat{\mathrm{EV}} \approx \operatorname{Var}_p(u)/N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, when the weights are not strongly
correlated with the payoffs. A rare history leaves most deals with tiny weight and a small $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$, so we
deal until $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ reaches a target, or a cap on $N$.

Every action is played out on the same deals (\emph{common random numbers}). Then
$\operatorname{Var}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{EV}}(a) - \widehat{\mathrm{EV}}(a')\big) = \operatorname{Var}
\widehat{\mathrm{EV}}(a) + \operatorname{Var}\widehat{\mathrm{EV}}(a') - 2\operatorname{Cov}$, and the covariance is
large and positive because the deal drives most of the payoff. Differences between actions, which are what decisions
depend on, are therefore much more precise than the levels. Their standard error is \eqref{eq:snis} applied to
$u_n(a) - u_n(a')$.

\section{Fitting a payoff model to observed play}\label{sec:fitting-maths}

The realization parameters $\theta$ are not observable, so we choose them so that the solved strategies resemble a set
of reference charts (\cref{sec:reference}). This section sets out the two objectives we use and how they relate to
models of boundedly rational play.

\begin{definition}[Weighted total-variation distance]\label{def:tv-distance}
At the node $h_s$ of a reference spot $s$, let $p = P(h_s)$, and give each hand class $k$ the weight $w_k = n_k\,
\reach^{\avgstrat}_p(h_s,k)$: its number of combinations $n_k$ times the probability that $p$'s own earlier actions
under $\avgstrat$ bring class $k$ to $h_s$. The distance between the strategy and the chart $\strat^{\mathrm{ref}}_s$
at $s$ is
\[
  D_s(\avgstrat) = \frac{\sum_k w_k\, \TV\big(\avgstrat(h_s,k),\, \strat^{\mathrm{ref}}_s(k)\big)}{\sum_k w_k}
  \in [0,1], \qquad \TV(x,y) = \tfrac12 \lVert x - y \rVert_1 .
\]
\end{definition}

Since $\TV(x,y)$ is the smallest probability, over couplings, that draws from $x$ and $y$ differ, $D_s$ is the
probability that the strategy and the chart choose different actions for a class drawn in proportion to $w_k$, under
the best coupling for each class. Classes the strategy never brings to the spot do not count, and frequent classes
count most. The reference spots are grouped into categories $c$ (opens, facing an open, facing a 3-bet, and so on).
The objective averages the spots within each category and then the categories, so that a category with many spots
does not dominate:
\[
  J(\theta) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal C|}\sum_{c \in \mathcal C} \frac{1}{|\mathcal S_c|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal S_c}
  D_s(\avgstrat_\theta) + \mu \sum_{p} \operatorname{dist}\big(o_p(\avgstrat_\theta), [l_p,u_p]\big),
\]
where $\avgstrat_\theta$ is the solved strategy under $\theta$, and the second term penalises open frequencies $o_p$
that leave sanity bands $[l_p,u_p]$ (\cref{sec:tuning}).

Each evaluation of $J$ is a full solve, so $J$ is expensive, noisy and offers no gradient. Pattern
search~\cite{hooke1961} needs only comparisons: it tries a step up and down each coordinate, moves when a step
improves by more than a few standard errors of the difference, and halves the steps when none does
(\cref{sec:tuning}). Every trial costs a solve, which caps the number of parameters that can be tuned.

\paragraph{Quantal response and the likelihood view.}
A cheaper objective treats the charts as noisy best responses. Fix every player's strategy, so that each action $a$
of class $k$ at spot $s$ has an expected value $\mathrm{EV}_\theta(s,k,a)$, and model play by the \emph{logit
response}
\[
  q_\theta(a \mid s,k) = \softmax\big(\mathrm{EV}_\theta(s,k,\cdot)/\tau\big)_a
  = \frac{\exp(\mathrm{EV}_\theta(s,k,a)/\tau)}{\sum_{a'} \exp(\mathrm{EV}_\theta(s,k,a')/\tau)},
\]
which tends to a best response as $\tau \to 0$ and to uniform play as $\tau \to \infty$. McKelvey and
Palfrey~\cite{mckelvey1995} define the quantal response equilibrium as a profile in which every player plays the logit
response to the others; holding the others fixed, as we do, is a one-sided version with no fixed point to compute. If
the chart mix $p_{s,k}$ is read as observed action frequencies, the weighted log-likelihood of the charts is
$\sum_{s,k} \omega_{s,k} \sum_a p_{s,k}(a) \log q_\theta(a\mid s,k)$. Maximising it is the same as minimising
\[
  \mathcal L(\theta) = \sum_{s,k} \omega_{s,k}\, \KL\big(p_{s,k} \,\big\|\, q_\theta(\cdot\mid s,k)\big), \qquad
  \frac{\partial\, \KL(p_{s,k}\,\|\,q_\theta)}{\partial\, \mathrm{EV}_\theta(s,k,a)} = \frac{q_\theta(a\mid s,k) - p_{s,k}(a)}{\tau},
\]
since the two differ by the charts' entropy, which does not depend on $\theta$. The weights $\omega_{s,k}$ are those
of \cref{def:tv-distance} with the category balancing of $J$. An $L^2$ penalty pulling $\theta$ towards a start point is
a Gaussian prior, which makes the minimiser a maximum a posteriori estimate. A complementary diagnostic is the chart's
\emph{regret} $\max_a \mathrm{EV}_\theta(s,k,a) - \sum_a p_{s,k}(a)\,\mathrm{EV}_\theta(s,k,a) \ge 0$: how much the
chart gives up against the best action, in big blinds.

Choosing the payoffs of a game so that observed play is (quantally) rational is \emph{inverse game theory}. Ling et
al.~\cite{ling2018} learn game parameters end to end by differentiating through the quantal response equilibrium of
normal- and extensive-form games. Our fixed-strategy fit (\cref{sec:fixed-fit}) is the simpler variant in which the
other players' strategies stay fixed, so the loss and its gradient need no equilibrium computation. The price is that
the fit cannot see how the other players would respond to a change in $\theta$ (\cref{sec:res-fit}).
