\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Motivation and scope}\label{sec:intro-scope}

Poker has long served as a benchmark for reasoning under imperfect information \cite{billings2002}. Unlike chess or Go, a player does not see the whole state of the game: the opponents' cards are hidden, so good play has to be randomised, and the value of an action depends on what the opponents believe as much as on the cards themselves. The last decade has seen a series of landmark results built on one family of algorithms, \emph{counterfactual regret minimisation} (CFR) \cite{zinkevich2007}. Heads-up limit hold'em was essentially solved by Cepheus \cite{bowling2015}. DeepStack \cite{moravcik2017} and Libratus \cite{brown2018} beat professional players at heads-up no-limit hold'em, and Pluribus \cite{brown2019pluribus} did so in the six-player game.

These systems need large compute budgets and solve the game street by street, re-solving subgames as play unfolds. Human players study a far smaller object: \emph{preflop charts}, tables of what to do with each starting hand before any community cards are dealt. Preflop is the decision every player faces in every hand, the stage where most hands end, and the one whose structure is simple enough to tabulate. Commercial tools publish such charts, computed by solving the full game on large clusters, but the charts come without the model behind them.

This report studies the preflop stage of six-player (\enquote{six-max}) no-limit hold'em cash games at a stack depth of 100 big blinds as a finite game in its own right. We
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item define a preflop-only game whose payoffs at the moment the flop would be dealt approximate the value of playing the rest of the hand, through a parametric \emph{realization model};
  \item solve it with external-sampling Monte Carlo CFR \cite{lanctot2009} with linear averaging \cite{brown2019discounted}, on a laptop, in about a minute;
  \item fit the realization model's parameters so that the solution matches published equilibrium charts \cite{pokertrainer}, and study a cheaper fitting method that avoids a solve per candidate;
  \item build an interactive trainer on top of the solution that grades a user's decisions by importance-sampled rollouts.
\end{enumerate}
Everything is implemented in our own software, \pf{}, written in C++20 with a raylib front end \cite{raylib} and the PokerHandEvaluator library \cite{phevaluator}, with Python tooling for the fitting.

The equilibrium we compute is an equilibrium of \emph{our} model, not of real poker. How close it gets to charts computed from the full game is therefore the central empirical question, and the realization model is where most of the modelling effort goes.

\section{A short introduction to six-max hold'em}\label{sec:intro-poker}

We assume no knowledge of poker; \textcite{sklansky1999} and \textcite{chen2006} are standard references for the game and its mathematics.

\paragraph{Cards and hands.} Each player is dealt two private \emph{hole cards} from a standard 52-card deck. Up to five \emph{community cards} are then dealt face up in three stages: the \emph{flop} (three cards), the \emph{turn} and the \emph{river} (one card each). A player's hand is the best five-card combination of their two hole cards and the five community cards. Hands are ranked from high card, one pair and two pair, through three of a kind (a \emph{set} when it uses a pocket pair), a straight (five consecutive ranks, the ace playing high or low, so A-2-3-4-5, the \emph{wheel}, counts) and a flush (five cards of one suit), up to a full house, four of a kind and a straight flush.

\paragraph{Betting.} There are four betting rounds: one before the flop (\emph{preflop}) and one after each of the flop, turn and river. In each round a player facing a bet may \emph{fold} (give up the hand and anything already put in), \emph{call} (match the bet) or \emph{raise} (increase it). In no-limit hold'em a raise may be any size up to the player's whole stack. A hand ends when all but one player have folded, who wins the pot uncontested, or at a showdown after the river, where the best hand wins.

\paragraph{Positions and blinds.} At a six-player table the seats are named by their position relative to the \emph{button} (\cref{fig:table}): under the gun (UTG, also called the lojack, LJ), hijack (HJ), cutoff (CO), button (BTN), small blind (SB) and big blind (BB). Before the cards are dealt, SB and BB post forced bets of $0.5$ and $1$ big blind (bb), which give the others something to win. Preflop, action starts with UTG and runs clockwise, so the blinds act last. On every later street the order changes: SB acts first and BTN last. Acting last postflop, being \emph{in position} (IP) against the remaining players, is a large advantage, since that player sees what everyone else did before deciding. All amounts in this report are in big blinds, and all stacks start at $100\bb$.

\paragraph{Preflop vocabulary.} The first voluntary raise is an \emph{open}; folding to it or calling it are the alternatives for the players behind. A re-raise of an open is a \emph{3-bet}, a re-raise of that a \emph{4-bet}, and so on (the blinds count as the first bet). Calling an open without having put money in yet is a \emph{cold call} or \emph{flat}; a 3-bet after an open and one or more calls is a \emph{squeeze}. A 4-bet called by a 3-bettor, or a 5-bet all-in, typically commits the whole stack.

\paragraph{Starting hands.} There are $\binom{52}{2} = 1326$ two-card \emph{combos}. Before the flop the suits are interchangeable, so combos fall into $169$ \emph{hand classes}: $13$ pocket pairs ($6$ combos each, e.g.\ AA), $78$ suited non-pairs ($4$ combos, e.g.\ AKs) and $78$ offsuit non-pairs ($12$ combos, e.g.\ AKo). We draw them on the usual $13\times 13$ grid: pairs on the diagonal, suited hands above it, offsuit hands below.

\paragraph{Equity and realization.} A hand's \emph{equity} against given opponent hands is its expected share of the pot if all remaining community cards were dealt and the hand went to showdown with no further betting. Equity is easy to compute but overstates what a hand actually wins: postflop betting forces folds, and a hand out of position, or one that rarely makes strong combinations, wins less than its equity. Players speak of how well a hand \emph{realises} its equity. Suited and connected hands make flushes and straights and realise well, offsuit hands with a weak kicker realise badly, and position helps everyone. This is the idea our payoff model formalises (\cref{sec:shares-maths,sec:realization}).

\section{The game we solve}\label{sec:intro-game}

\paragraph{Players and stakes.} Six players with $100\bb$ each, blinds $0.5/1$, stacks reset every hand. The game ends at the flop: whenever betting closes with two or more players left, we stop and pay out according to a model of the remaining play (\cref{sec:shares-maths}).

\paragraph{Actions and sizes.} Every decision is a subset of $\{F, C, R\}$ (fold, call, raise) with one raise size per level, chosen to match the reference charts \cite{pokertrainer}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item opens are to $2.5\bb$, or $3\bb$ from SB;
  \item 3-bets are $3\times$ the open in position and $5\times$ from the blinds, plus one open size per caller (a squeeze);
  \item 4-bets are $2\times$ the 3-bet in position and $2.55\times$ out of position;
  \item a 5-bet is all-in, and facing an all-in a player may only call or fold.
\end{itemize}
Raise totals are rounded to $0.1\bb$. For example, BB 3-bets a BTN open to $12.5\bb$, and BTN can 4-bet to $25\bb$. \Cref{app:game} lists every size.

\paragraph{Cold calling.} Players may call an open, and later players may overcall it, but once a 3-bet is in, players who have not yet put money in may only fold or raise. A player who cold-called the open may also call a squeeze. Limping (calling the big blind without raising) is not allowed.

\paragraph{Rake.} As in the online games the reference charts are computed for, the house takes $5\%$ of the pot, capped at $4\bb$, but only when a flop is dealt or an all-in is called (\enquote{no flop, no drop}). The game is therefore negative-sum for the players.

\paragraph{Size.} With these rules the betting tree has \num{7499} decision nodes and \num{8529} terminal histories: \num{1703} where everyone else folds, \num{5797} called all-ins and \num{1029} that reach a flop, \num{378} of them with three or more players. The longest line has $19$ actions. A fragment of the tree is shown in \cref{fig:tree-fragment}. With one information set per decision node and hand class, a player has $\num{7499}\times 169 \approx \num{1.27e6}$ information sets in total across all seats.

\section{Abstractions}\label{sec:intro-abstractions}

Real no-limit hold'em is far too large to solve directly: even heads-up it has more than $10^{160}$ decision points \cite{johanson2013}. Our game differs from the real game in three ways.

\paragraph{Action abstraction.} One raise size per level, no limping, and no cold calls of 3-bets. These match the reference charts' own rules, so they are restrictions shared with the target rather than errors relative to it. Abstraction can have counter-intuitive effects on the quality of an equilibrium \cite{waugh2009}, which is one reason we keep the rules identical to the charts'.

\paragraph{Card abstraction.} Strategies depend on the hand class, not the combo. Preflop, this loses nothing: relabelling suits maps the game onto itself, so an equilibrium can be taken suit-symmetric \cite{gilpin2007}. The solver still deals real combos from one deck, so \emph{card removal} (holding an ace makes the opponents' aces less likely) is modelled exactly.

\paragraph{Payoff abstraction.} This is the large one. We do not play the flop, turn and river. Instead, a terminal where the flop would be dealt pays each live player a share of the pot given by their equity, scaled by a \emph{realization factor} that depends on the hand, the player's role (position, caller or raiser) and the depth of the pot. The factors have a dozen or so parameters, which we fit to the charts (\cref{sec:realization,sec:tuning}).

\section{Related work}\label{sec:intro-related}

CFR was introduced by \textcite{zinkevich2007}, and its Monte Carlo variants by \textcite{lanctot2009}. CFR\textsuperscript{+} \cite{tammelin2014} solved heads-up limit hold'em \cite{bowling2015}, and linear and discounted weighting schemes \cite{brown2019discounted} speed up convergence further. \textcite{neller2013} give an accessible tutorial. For more than two players CFR has no equilibrium guarantee, but it still removes dominated actions \cite{gibson2014} and produces strong play in practice: Pluribus \cite{brown2019pluribus} used Monte Carlo CFR with linear weighting for its six-player blueprint. Function approximation has also been combined with CFR \cite{brown2019deep, moravcik2017}.

Preflop-only games have been studied in the form of \emph{push/fold} (all-in or fold) games for short stacks, where the equilibrium can be computed exactly for two players and approximately for three \cite{ganzfried2008}. Our game is much richer: deep stacks, four raise levels, cold calls and squeezes, with postflop value replaced by a model. Fitting payoff parameters so that observed play becomes rational is a case of inverse game theory, related to learning game parameters from equilibrium play \cite{ling2018} and to quantal-response models of behaviour \cite{mckelvey1995}.

\section{Contributions and outline}\label{sec:intro-outline}

The contributions of this report are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a precise definition of a six-player preflop game with rake and a realization-adjusted payoff model built from hand features (suitedness, connectivity including wheel straights, pairs, position, initiative, domination and pot depth);
  \item a fast external-sampling MCCFR solver with lazy linear weighting and lock-free threading, checked against an exactly solvable heads-up game;
  \item a fit of the model to published charts, a solve with the charts locked that exposes where model and charts disagree, and a study of a cheap fixed-strategy fitting method, including why it fails where it does;
  \item an interactive trainer that grades decisions with importance-sampled rollouts and standard errors.
\end{itemize}

\Cref{ch:maths} develops the mathematics from first principles: extensive-form games and equilibria, regret, CFR and its Monte Carlo variants, the estimators we use, and the statistics of fitting. \Cref{ch:method} describes the model and its implementation: the encoding of the game, equity, the realization features, the solver, the reference charts, both fitting methods and the trainer. \Cref{ch:results} reports correctness checks, convergence, agreement with the charts, where the model and the charts disagree, and the outcome of the fitting study. The appendices list the bet sizes, the fitted parameters and further range charts.
